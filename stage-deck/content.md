# Content

Read before writing any headline, description, heading, or body text. This file
governs the words themselves; arrangement is `archetypes.md`, drawing is
`figures.md`.

The words are agreed with the user before anything is drawn (`SKILL.md` §0,
gate 3). This file is what the list you bring to that gate is checked against.

---

## 1. Headline and description

Every beat opens with a **headline** (its claim) and, in a `standalone` deck, a
**description** under it. Together they must let a reader who skips the body
still take the point.

### The headline

A headline's job is **retrieval, then argument** — a reader skimming for one
topic must find it from the headline alone.

- **A full sentence that states the point.** Subject and finding, in the
  material's own terms. "Refunds under $50 should be approved on the call, not
  by a supervisor" is a headline. "Small refunds" is a label, and "Rethinking
  small refunds" is a topic.
- **Front-load the noun a reader would scan for.** The search term appears in
  the first few words, not after a wind-up.
- **Use the material's terminology, never a synonym for variety.** A reader
  hunting a term the source uses must hit it verbatim.
- **No headline may need its body to decode.** If it reads as wordplay before
  the body and informative only after, invert it: state the finding plainly and
  let the body earn it.
- **Prefer the specific quantity, direction, or threshold** over an abstraction
  of it — **but only where the number is the finding.** A dozen count-led
  headlines become a drumbeat and the precision stops signifying. Evidence the
  audience can read elsewhere supports the argument; it is rarely the argument.
- **Nothing sits above or beside it.** No eyebrow, no number, no chapter label.
- **Length is a content constraint.** Two lines at most: about 110 characters
  in `standalone`, about 60 in `presented` (`SKILL.md` §2). Those are ceilings.
  The right length is the shortest sentence that still says the whole point.
- **The index test governs the whole deck:** the headlines, listed bare in
  order, are the deck's table of contents. Every beat must be findable from that
  list alone, and the list must still carry the argument. The verifier prints
  the list; read it as a reader would.

### The description

- **One to three sentences that add what the headline cannot hold:** the
  reason, the condition, the consequence, or the one thing a reader needs before
  the figure makes sense.
- **It never restates the headline.** Test it: strike from the description
  every word the headline already says. If no sentence is left, the description
  is the headline said twice. Rewrite it or delete it.
- **It never describes the figure.** "The diagram shows three regions feeding
  one warehouse" is a caption for something the reader is already looking at.
  A description may *introduce* a figure by saying what question it answers.
- **It does not end where the headline ends.** A headline closing on "not on
  price alone" above a description opening "price is only one part" is
  one idea delivered twice. Give the headline the claim and the description the
  next thing.

### Every piece of text adds something

The same test runs across the whole slide. The headline, the description, the
figure's labels, the notes beside it and the evidence note each say something
the others do not. Look for these by name:

- a label inside a figure that repeats a word from the headline above it;
- a note that explains what the figure plainly shows;
- an evidence note that repeats the description;
- a closing line that summarises the three points above it;
- **a label that says nothing.** "One trusted place to track an order" could
  caption any tool in any deck. A label has to be able to be wrong: say what the
  thing does, for whom, or what it replaces.

### Register shifts by role

- **Chapter titles name territory** — short noun phrases, no finding.
- **Body headlines report findings** — the contract above, in full.
- **The title slide carries the deck's subject**, short and plain, with a
  subline saying what the deck covers. A title is a name. It does not announce
  its own genre ("A point of view on…", "Rethinking…").

## 2. Distill, then test each claim

What the user tells you, and what an outline says, are **input**. Neither is
slide copy.

### Distill

- **Never quote the user onto a slide.** Their message explains what they want
  to a collaborator. The slide speaks to the audience, in the deck's register.
  Work out what they mean and write that.
- **A list of examples is not content.** When the user gives five examples of a
  thing, they are showing you the category. Name the category, and keep at most
  one example if the audience needs it. The rest are for the conversation,
  not the page.
- **Do not overcorrect.** Asked to be more specific, add the specific thing;
  do not replace a clear sentence with a dense one. Asked to be less pithy,
  remove the pith; do not double the length.
- **An outline's sentence earns its place like any other.** It may be a first
  draft and it may be machine-written. Fluent is not the same as true.

### Test each claim

Run these on every claim before it reaches the gate. A claim that fails one is
either fixed or raised with the user as a doubt.

1. **Is this the real problem?** "Customers cannot find the returns page"
   sounds like a problem and is not the one that costs anything; the real one
   is that every return waits on a phone call to approve it. Ask what actually
   goes wrong, for whom, and what it costs them.
2. **Could it be false?** "Support cost rises with the number of tickets" is
   always true, so it tells the reader nothing. A claim worth a slide is one a
   reasonable person could dispute.
3. **Would someone who does this work agree?** If the audience includes a
   practitioner, a claim that is slightly wrong about their job costs the deck
   its standing.
4. **Does it say who and what?** "Onboarding nobody owns" gestures. "No named
   owner for the first-week checklist, and no check that accounts exist" names
   two things.
5. **Is it the deck's to say?** A request for budget or sponsorship, a
   criticism of a named team, a promise about a date: check that the user wants
   it on the page and not carried in conversation.

## 3. Sub-heads and blocks

Sub-heads are headlines one level down; the whole contract applies at that
level.

- **Every block leads with its own assertion** — subject and finding, not a
  category label.
- **Parallel construction, and parallel in *kind*.** Items side by side take the
  same grammatical shape, land in the same length band, and are the same sort of
  thing. A condition, a shift, and a dependency are not three parallel items even
  if each is one line; two options and a caveat is two items and a footnote.
- **No block repeats another**, and no block re-lists what a figure beside it
  already labels.
- **The skim path survives alone:** headlines and sub-heads read top to bottom
  carry the argument without the detail text.
- **One idea per block.** A block whose second sentence changes subject is two
  blocks.

## 4. Support lines, strips, and labels

- **A support line extends the claim; it never restates it.**
- **A strip item is a labelled fact: label and content, both present.** A row of
  labels with nothing beneath them is scaffolding without cargo — cut the strip or
  give each item its payload.
- **Labels in the meta register locate; text in the body registers asserts.** Do
  not promote a locator to an assertion by styling alone.
- **A label names what the reader is looking at, in the words they would use.**
  "Call the customer back" reads; "callback performed" does not.

## 5. Evidence notes

The evidence note (the basis) is the stage's fine print: where the claim comes
from, how far it has been tested, declared arrangements, scope limits. It has a
content contract, not just a length cap:

- **An evidence note is required whenever the stage carries** a declared
  measurement arrangement, a derived or synthesised value, a schematic geometry,
  a contested figure, or a claim whose standing the reader would ask about
  (tested, observed, built, planned). Otherwise omit it.
- **It may qualify the body; it may never carry the body.** An argument demoted to
  the evidence note to make a beat fit is a coverage failure — the
  pressure-relief move is banned by name.
- ≤ 2 lines at full width — **mechanically enforced**: its ink must stay inside
  its band, and a note crossing the foot hairline is a build failure, not a
  style choice. Over two lines it is doing body work.
- **The declared pointer.** Between carrying an argument and omitting it there is
  a third register: one clause acknowledging a real cost or story the deck
  does not treat, placed at the beat where its subject appears. Use it for a
  material omission a careful reader would notice, and never as a gesture toward
  an argument the deck should either make or drop.

## 6. Voice

- Assertions in the present tense, active voice, no hedging the source does not
  hedge.
- **Plain wording, in full sentences.** Three failures to check for by name:
  - **The slogan.** A line built to be quoted: a neat reversal, a pun on the
    product's name, a three-beat rhythm. It reads as cleverness and costs the
    deck its seriousness with a senior reader.
  - **The stock phrase.** Wording that could sit in any deck on any subject and
    that a reader recognises as filler. If a phrase arrived without your having
    to think about it, replace it with the specific thing.
  - **The stilted construction.** "The supervisor is then brought in for the
    cases that need it" where "The supervisor handles the rest" says it. Read
    each sentence aloud. If you would not say it to a colleague, rewrite it.
- **Keep the subject's technical terms.** An expert audience is not helped by
  simpler vocabulary, and a deck that avoids the field's own words looks as if
  its author does not know them. What a reader outside the field lacks is how
  the parts fit together, and that is the figure's job.
- **Never anthropomorphise the deck** — no "this slide shows," no "here we see."
  The stage shows; the text asserts.
- Numbers as numerals; units in the meta register's style; ranges with an en dash.
- Sentence case everywhere except the meta register's uppercase.
